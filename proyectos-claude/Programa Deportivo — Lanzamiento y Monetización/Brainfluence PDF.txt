About the book
In "Brainfluence," Roger Dooley dives into the fascinating
intersection of neuroscience and marketing, revealing how
understanding the brain can elevate consumer behavior and
influence decision-making. By unpacking the science behind
why we buy and what drives our choices, Dooley equips
marketers and business leaders with cutting-edge insights that
tap into the subconscious mind. From the impact of sensory
experiences to the power of emotions, this book is a
compelling exploration of the neuropsychological principles
that shape our everyday purchases. As you turn the pages,
prepare to discover not just the art of persuasion, but the
science that underlies it, empowering you to craft more
effective strategies and forge deeper connections with your
audience.

About the author
Roger Dooley is a renowned author, speaker, and consultant in
the fields of neuroscience and marketing, celebrated for his
ability to bridge the gap between scientific insights and
practical business applications. With a diverse background that
includes a successful career in marketing and
entrepreneurship, Dooley draws on both his academic
knowledge and real-world experience to explore how
understanding the brain can enhance customer engagement
and decision-making. As the founder of the popular blog
Neuromarketing, he has established himself as a thought
leader in the intersection of behavioral science and marketing
strategy. His book, "Brainfluence," distills complex concepts
into actionable strategies, helping businesses harness the
power of neuroscience to influence consumer behavior
effectively.

Chapter 1: Sell to 95 Percent of Your Customer’s
Brain
Understanding that 95% of thoughts, emotions, and learning
occur subconsciously is essential for marketers, as
highlighted by Harvard professor Gerald Zaltman. While the
exact percentage is debated, it is widely accepted that

subconscious processes significantly influence
decision-making. Research shows people might make
decisions up to eight seconds before they consciously realize
it, emphasizing that customers often cannot articulate their
purchasing reasons.
Brainfluence Takeaway: Stop Selling to 5 Percent of
Your Customer’s Brain
Marketers tend to focus predominantly on rational
messaging, but should instead prioritize connecting with
customers' emotions and subconscious needs. Rational
arguments, while useful for justifying decisions, are less
effective in driving sales.
SECTION ONE: Price and Product
Chapter 2: The “Ouch!” of Paying
Neuromarketing research points to the brain's pain center
activating during purchases. Studies reveal that individual
pricing can lead to greater consumer pain compared to
bundled pricing, which minimizes perceived unfairness and

total pain associated with spending.
Brainfluence Takeaway: Minimum Pain, Maximum
Sales
To enhance sales, pricing strategies must appear fair, and
bundling products can help reduce pain felt during
transactions. Consumer payment options should alleviate
immediate financial stress, enabling better purchasing
decisions.
Chapter 3: Don’t Sell Like a Sushi Chef
Pricing strategies that break down costs into individual units
can cause psychological discomfort, as seen with sushi
pricing models. Experiments show that fixed pricing—such
as monthly subscriptions—can reduce perceived pain and
increase consumer attraction.
Brainfluence Takeaway: Avoid Multiple Pain Points
Marketers should limit the number of individual pain points
in transactions. A single pricing approach for items usually
sold separately can boost sales and margins, appealing to

consumers' sensitivity to purchasing pain.
Chapter 4: Picturing Money
Priming is a significant concept, where subtle cues can
unconsciously influence behavior. Money-related cues can
provoke selfish tendencies and affect consumer interactions
and preferences. Advertisers should carefully consider how
they introduce financial imagery in campaigns.
Brainfluence Takeaway: Use Money Cues Wisely
Money cues should align with the emotional context of the
product. They can be effective in promoting self-interest but
may undermine campaigns focused on altruism or gifting.
Caution is advised when incorporating financial elements in
emotionally-driven advertisements.

Example
Key Point:Subconscious Decision-Making
Example:Imagine you're shopping for a new phone. As
you browse through options, you feel a strong attraction
to a particular model, perhaps based on its sleek design
or the positive feelings it evokes. Even before you
consciously weigh the pros and cons, your subconscious
is already steering you towards that choice, illustrating
how emotional connections can drive decisions far more
powerfully than logical reasoning.

Critical Thinking
Key Point:Understanding the Subconscious in
Marketing
Critical Interpretation:The emphasis on engaging the
subconscious for effective marketing is compelling;
however, it's crucial to understand that not all
psychological theories universally apply. While the
argument that 95% of customer processing occurs
subconsciously can guide marketing strategies, it's
worth considering perspectives like those of Daniel
Kahneman, who presents a more nuanced view in
'Thinking, Fast and Slow.' This suggests that consumers'
rational thinking also plays a pivotal role, thereby
challenging the idea that emotions must predominate.
Therefore, while appealing to subconscious feelings can
be powerful, marketers should not entirely dismiss
logical reasoning, and a balanced approach tailored to
target demographics may yield better results.

Chapter 5: Anchors Aweigh!
This chapter explores the concept of "anchoring" in pricing
and how it influences consumer perceptions and decisions. It
delves into different scenarios of pricing and how anchor
points can be dynamically set or reset in the consumer's
mind.
Anchoring in Consumer Decisions
- When making purchasing decisions, consumers assess
fairness based on an anchor price they have in mind—a
reference point that can dramatically influence their
perception of value.

- Buying pain is heightened when the price seems too high,
making understanding anchoring critical for effective
marketing.
Fluctuating Anchors: Gasoline Prices
- Gasoline prices frequently change, leading consumers to
reset their anchor points. For example, a price that once felt
exorbitant can later feel reasonable as consumers adjust to
new prices.
Sticky Anchors: Real Estate
- Unlike gasoline, home buyers take longer to adapt to new
price anchors after relocating. This stickiness in anchor
points leads to inconsistent pricing decisions.
Unfamiliar Products and Anchor Formation
- For items that are infrequently purchased, initial prices can
create anchor points that shape future purchase assessments.
An example given is a big-screen TV, where an initial price
serves as a reference for other options.

Irrational Anchors
- Research shows that anchoring can be influenced by
seemingly unrelated factors, such as random numbers.
Higher arbitrary numbers can lead to consumers being
willing to pay more for products due to unintended anchoring
effects.
Establishing Anchors
- Marketers should be strategic about the prices they set,
particularly for new products without established anchor
points. A higher initial price can create a favorable anchor for
future offers. Apple's iPhone marketing demonstrates this
strategy effectively.
Nonsense Anchors and Testing
- Marketers are encouraged to experiment with anchoring
strategies, even using nonsensical anchors, to test effects on
consumer willingness to pay.
Infomercial Strategies

- Successful infomercials establish high anchor prices before
presenting discounts, making the final offer appear
particularly attractive through relative pricing.
Chapter 6: Wine, Prices, and Expectations
- Consumer perception of a product, such as wine, can be
influenced by its price, where higher-priced wine is often
rated as tasting better, even if it's the same as cheaper
options.
- This phenomenon underscores the importance for marketers
to recognize that perceptions can shape product experiences
post-purchase.
Chapter 7: Be Precise With Prices
- Using precise pricing (e.g., $499.99 instead of $500) can
positively influence perceived value, leading customers to
regard products as higher quality.
- Research indicates that odd pricing maintains a perception
of precision, thereby drawing in consumers more effectively.
Chapter 8: Decoy Products and Pricing

- Introducing a less attractive product as a "decoy" can
enhance sales of a preferred item. The presence of various
options allows consumers to make favorable comparisons.
- Real-world examples illustrate how decoys can be
strategically employed to drive sales.
Chapter 9: How About a Compromise?
- Offering high-end products can shift the consumer's
perception of mid-range offerings as a reasonable
compromise, boosting their sales despite the premium
product's weak performance.
- Utilizing decoy products in retail, like expensive menu
items, can create favorable comparisons that enhance sales of
other items.

Example
Key Point:Anchoring effects can dramatically alter
your perception of value in purchasing decisions.
Example:Imagine you're shopping for a new
smartphone. You initially see one priced at $1,200,
which feels unreasonably high. Then you find another at
$800, and suddenly it seems like a fantastic deal
because of what you first encountered. This ‘anchoring’
effect influences your assessment of the $800 phone's
worth and can lead you to make a purchase you might
have otherwise skipped, reinforcing the importance of
initial pricing perceptions in guiding consumer
behavior.

Critical Thinking
Key Point:The role of anchoring in shaping
consumer perceptions and decisions is significant,
but its implications warrant skepticism.
Critical Interpretation:While 'Brainfluence' by Roger
Dooley emphasizes that anchor prices heavily influence
how consumers perceive value and make purchasing
decisions, one must critically consider that this concept
of anchoring may not always translate to all consumers
or contexts universally. For example, anchoring effects
can vary based on individual circumstances, cultural
context, and prior experiences with products, thus
challenging the notion that it will uniformly dictate
consumer behavior. Empirical studies have indicated
that while anchoring can have strong effects, they are
not infallible and may not apply equally across all
products or price ranges (North, A. C., & Hargreaves,
D. J., 2008, “The Effect of Price on Consumers' Price
Judgments”). Additionally, consumers can also rely on
other cues, such as brand reputation or previous
experiences, which may overtake anchoring in
determining perceived product value. Therefore, while
the anchoring effect is a powerful tool for marketers, it

should be used cautiously, acknowledging its potential
limitations.

Chapter 3 Summary
Cutting Choices to Boost Sales
 
Consumers often believe they want a wide variety of choices,
evidenced by the multitude of products available in stores.
However, research from Columbia University indicates that
too many options can actually decrease sales. In a study
involving gourmet jams, a limited selection of 6 jams
resulted in a 30% purchase rate compared to only 3% from a
selection of 24. This phenomenon, known as choice fatigue,
shows that an overload of options can overwhelm consumers
and hinder their purchasing decisions.
Finding the Choice Sweet Spot
 
Marketers should aim for an optimal balance of
choices—enough to satisfy consumer preferences but not so
many that it causes confusion. Testing different selection
sizes is crucial rather than relying on assumptions. Reducing

the number of product options can enhance sales, as
evidenced by Walmart and Procter & Gamble's experiences
after cutting back on choices.
Guiding Consumer Choices
 
Assisting customers with their choices can significantly
influence purchasing behavior. The Columbia study noted
that guidance from salespeople, who could ask questions and
make recommendations, could lead to higher sales in large
selection environments. In self-service settings,
well-organized displays and informative signage can attract
customer attention and facilitate decision-making.
Online Shopping Considerations
 
Online retailers have an advantage in offering broader
selections through various mechanisms such as
recommendation engines and reviews. However, a poorly
organized array can deter customers. Strategies that
differentiate products based on meaningful variations are
essential.

Research emphasizes that repeated sensory experiences with

Maintaining Brand Associations

Critical Thinking
Key Point:The Effectiveness of Audio Branding
Critical Interpretation:While Dooley posits that

preferences may heavily influence effectiveness.
Additionally, certain critiques suggest that excessive
reliance on sound may detract from visual branding
efforts, ultimately leading to a dissonance in consumer
perception. Research further supports that brand
recognition is multi-faceted, with visual elements often
playing a dominant role in consumer decision-making,
as articulated in studies by Aaker (1997) and other
branding scholars.

Chapter 5 Summary

Who Needs Attention?
 
Advertising effectiveness is often gauged by whether it
captures and retains viewer attention. While attention is
beneficial, studies suggest that ads can still influence
consumers even when they are not consciously noticed.
These effects stem from low-involvement processing, where
brand messages are absorbed unconsciously.
Low Attention, No Attention
 
Research indicates that consumers can be influenced without
full engagement. Dr. Robert Heath's work reveals that ads
can have an impact without being consciously processed,
contradicting traditional beliefs about ad effectiveness.
"Ignored" TV Commercials
 
Experiments with TV commercials show that even those who
pay little attention to ads experience a shift in brand
perception. Various levels of exposure—high attention, low
attention, and even no recall—resulted in positive brand
shifts without conscious recognition of the ads.

Fast-Forward Branding
 
Even fast-forwarded ads can have an impact. Studies reveal
that consumers who fast-forward through commercials are
still able to recall brand information, particularly if they have
previously watched the ads at regular speed.
Branding Without Seeing
 
Consumers process brand imagery using both conscious and
subconscious pathways. Research into "blindsight" shows
that even those with visual impairments can respond to visual
stimuli. Brands can leverage this subconscious processing for
effective marketing.
Familiarity Breeds Likeability
 
The mere exposure effect demonstrates that repeated
unconscious exposure to a brand can enhance likeability.
Striking a balance between brand visibility and positive
associations is crucial for reinforcing brand preference.
Brainfluence Takeaway: “No Attention” Doesn’t
Mean “No Results”

 
Even unnoticed ads can influence consumer behavior. Brands
should seek continual visibility and positive associations,
using strategies such as product placement and sponsorships
to embed their presence in consumers' lives.
Passion for Hire
 
Creating a "passion brand" requires hiring passionate
employees who can transmit enthusiasm for the brand. Such
employees enhance customer experiences through genuine
engagement, fostering loyalty and advocacy.
Create an Enemy
 
Establishing a rival brand can galvanize consumer loyalty.
Brands that create a distinctive "us versus them" mentality,
like Apple, build passionate followings and deepen brand
attachment.
Us Versus Them
 
Psychological studies—such as the Tajfel
Experiment—suggest that people quickly develop loyalty to

groups, which can be harnessed in branding strategies to
foster community and brand advocacy.
Brainfluence Takeaway: Make Your Customers Feel
Like Members of a Group
 
Brands should strive to cultivate a sense of community
among customers, differentiating them from competitors to
enhance loyalty and generate brand evangelism.
Use Paper for Emotion
 
Despite the rise of digital media, print can evoke stronger
emotional responses due to its tangibility. Research shows
physical media is processed differently, leading to deeper
emotional connections with advertisements.
Vivid Print Images Change Memory
 
Print ads using vivid imagery can create strong impressions,
sometimes leading to false memories of consumers having
experienced a product. This emphasizes the importance of
high-quality visuals in print advertising.

Brainfluence Takeaway: Use Vivid Images in Print
 
The use of powerful imagery in print advertising can enhance
positive consumer experiences and impressions of products,
underscoring print's relevance in a digital era.

Example
Key Point:Engagement Level and Brand Awareness
Example:Imagine flipping through a magazine while
waiting at the dentist's office, barely paying attention
because you're preoccupied with your thoughts. Yet, an
eye-catching ad draws your gaze for just a split second.
That ad, despite your distracted state, plants a subtle
seed in your mind. Later, you find yourself preferring
that brand when making a choice at the store, not even
recalling seeing the ad earlier. This phenomenon
showcases that even fleeting moments of exposure can
shape your brand preferences, emphasizing the power of
low-involvement processing in advertising.

Critical Thinking
Key Point:The efficacy of unnoticed advertising
Critical Interpretation:One critical point in the chapter
summary of 'Brainfluence' focuses on the notion that
advertising can influence consumer behavior without
the audience's conscious attention. This perspective,
suggested by Dr. Robert Heath's findings, explores the
potential of low-involvement processing, whereby brand
messages can be absorbed subconsciously. However, it's
important to critically examine this viewpoint, as it
challenges traditional ad effectiveness models that
prioritize conscious acknowledgment of branding
efforts. Critics may argue that the lack of conscious
engagement might lead to weak brand recall and
diminished loyalty over time, which could contradict the
author's assertion that 'no attention' doesn't equate to 'no
results.' Source material from contrasting studies, such
as those published in the Journal of Consumer Research,
could offer evidence that highlights the limitations of
subconscious processing in developing long-term
consumer loyalty.

Chapter 8 Summary: Rewarding Loyalty and
Building Trust
Loyalty Programs Effectiveness
Loyalty programs are abundant in various retail settings, like
coffee shops and grocery stores, and they effectively
motivate consumer behavior. Studies show that consumers
are more likely to use credit cards with appealing rewards,
especially when the underlying product is competitive.
However, factors such as brand loyalty, perceived value of
rewards, and purchase frequency play crucial roles in the
success of these programs.
Loyalty Point Power
Research indicates even irrelevant loyalty points can
influence purchasing decisions, demonstrating that
consumers respond to the mere presence of reward values.
For instance, Starbucks and Panera Bread leverage electronic

loyalty programs, emphasizing customer engagement but
lacking visible progress indicators. 
Brainfluence Takeaway: Offer Loyalty Rewards
To maximize loyalty program effectiveness, businesses
should actively engage customers and highlight their
progress toward rewards. The addition of attractive point
offers can entice consumers to choose a brand over
competitors.
The Psychology of Goal Gradient
Following the goal gradient hypothesis, the desire to reach
rewards increases as consumers perceive themselves getting
closer to the goal. Experiments reveal that perceived
progress, even from a partially filled loyalty card, boosts
purchase frequency. 
Brainfluence Takeaway: Give a Head Start
Provide customers with a sense of progress from the outset
by partially filling their loyalty goals, enhancing their
motivation to complete the program.

The Importance of Contact Time
In today's productivity-driven environment, companies often
prioritize efficiency, sometimes at the expense of building
customer relationships. Studies reveal that increased contact
time, whether through direct interaction or timely feedback,
fosters trust and satisfaction among various consumer
groups, including convicted felons, venture capitalists, and
injured patients.
Brainfluence Takeaway: Quality Contact Time
Counts
Investing time in customer interactions builds relationships
and fosters loyalty, particularly during challenging situations.
Building Trust Through Advertising
Adding a simple statement encouraging customers to trust a
brand significantly boosts trust levels, showing that explicit
reminders of trust can influence consumer perceptions
positively.

Brainfluence Takeaway: Tell ’em to Trust You
Explicitly stating that customers can trust your brand can
enhance perception of its fairness and quality.
Demonstrating Trust to Gain Trust
Exhibiting trust in customers can foster reciprocal trust and
strengthen relationships. Companies can show trust by
minimizing restrictions or providing trial products without
excessive scrutiny.
Brainfluence Takeaway: Show Trust to Get Trust
Encouraging trust relationships involves demonstrating faith
in customers while maintaining safety nets for the business.
Conclusion: Human Interaction Matters
Despite the growing prevalence of remote transactions,
face-to-face interactions provide a unique opportunity for
richer engagement, favoring successful relationships rooted
in personal connection.

Example
Key Point:Importance of Perceived Progress in
Loyalty Programs
Example:Imagine you just joined a coffee shop’s loyalty
program. As you enjoy your first cup, you see your card
already marked with a star for signing up. This small
gesture makes you feel you’re well on your way to
earning a free beverage. The excitement grows each
time you pay, as you anticipate the reward just within
reach. By offering you an immediate sense of progress,
even a single point can significantly increase your
motivation to return, illustrating how perceived
advancement in loyalty programs can drive repeat
business.

purchases. Strategies include displaying low stock warnings
such as “Only 4 left in stock.” Dynamic update messages and
a sense of urgency can encourage immediate action,

Groupon.

When targeting older demographics, marketers should
prioritize simplicity due to the declining ability of older
brains to suppress distractions. Techniques include using
straightforward messaging, uncluttered layouts, and avoiding
distractions to enhance memory retention and buyer
engagement.
Encouraging Ownership Imagery to Drive Sales

Research reveals that even when customers can’t physically
touch a product, prompting them to visualize ownership can
increase their willingness to purchase. E-commerce strategies
may include using leading questions in product descriptions
or displaying items in context (e.g., showing tires fitted on a
customer’s own vehicle).
Logo Placement and Brand Visibility
Studies indicate that logos placed in the lower right corner of
advertisements often go unnoticed, while more effective
placements are found in the lower middle part of layouts.
Placing the brand where viewers are likely to be emotionally
engaged can enhance brand recall and engagement.
Humanizing Computer Interactions
To improve user experience with automated systems,
businesses should leverage social strategies that make
machines feel more relatable. Techniques include creating
team allegiances, positioning interfaces as allies, and
specializing functions. By treating computers as people,
businesses can enhance customer satisfaction, making
automated interactions feel more personal.

What is anchoring in the context of consumer pricing?
Answer:Anchoring is the cognitive bias where
individuals rely heavily on the first piece of
information they encounter (the anchor) when
making decisions about value. In pricing, this means
that consumers compare the prices of products to a
reference price (the anchor) stored in their memory,
which can significantly influence their willingness to
buy.
2.Question

Answer:Gasoline prices fluctuate frequently, causing
consumers to reset their anchor points quickly. For instance,
after seeing prices rise to $4 per gallon, consumers may
initially feel pain but eventually adapt. Subsequently, if they

see gas priced at $3.99, they perceive it as a bargain, as their
mental anchor has shifted to accept the new higher price
range.

Answer:Dan Ariely's research demonstrated that random,
unrelated numbers can unconsciously influence pricing
decisions. Participants who thought of higher random
numbers (like the last two digits of their Social Security
number) expressed a willingness to pay significantly higher
prices for various items, showing that anchors can be
manipulated intuitively.

reduced the price significantly, consumers viewed it as an
attractive deal, driving higher demand.

measurement of value, making them less likely to view the
product as overpriced.

Answer:Decoy products are intentionally less attractive

desirable product, making it stand out as a better value.

more highly, leading to a greater likelihood of purchase.

Therefore, providing opportunities for customers to interact
with a product can greatly improve sales success.
5.Question
What is the connection between difficulty in purchasing
luxury items and consumer perception?
Answer:Making the buying process challenging for luxury
products can increase buyer commitment due to cognitive
dissonance. When consumers expend effort to obtain a highly
coveted item, they tend to justify the trouble by reinforcing
their belief in its exceptional value. This contrasts with
general merchandise, where ease of purchase usually
enhances sales.
6.Question
What role does nonverbal communication play in
marketing effectiveness?
Answer:Nonverbal communication, such as gestures and
body language, significantly impacts how marketing
messages are received. If a spokesperson's physical actions
align with their verbal messages, this enhances the message's

effectiveness. Conversely, if there is inconsistency between
word and gesture, it can create confusion or distrust,
detracting from the overall impact of the marketing effort.



Chapter 19 | 19| Q&A
1.Question
What is priming by order and why is it significant in
marketing?
Answer:Priming by order refers to the phenomenon
where the sequence in which information is
presented influences the perception of that
information. In marketing, this is significant because
it shows that the order of presenting messages can
dramatically affect how consumers view a product
or candidate. For example, focus groups were found
to have a more negative impression of a candidate
when a poorly received speech was shown first,
rather than leading with positive testimonials.
2.Question
How do emotional appeals in advertising outperform
rational ones?
Answer:Emotional appeals in advertising often lead to better
consumer engagement and effectiveness compared to purely

rational messages. According to the IPA data, purely
emotional campaigns performed twice as well in boosting
profitability compared to campaigns that relied solely on
rational persuasion. This is due to the brain’s ability to
process emotional stimuli more quickly and effectively,
making ads that connect on an emotional level more
memorable.
3.Question
Why is the first impression so critical when it comes to
websites?
Answer:First impressions on websites are crucial because
users can make their judgment about the appeal of a site in
just 50 milliseconds. This instantaneous impression can
heavily influence their continued engagement. If the first
impression is negative, users are likely to dismiss subsequent
information, regardless of its quality.
4.Question
What role does the golden mean play in design and how
can it impact user engagement?

Answer:The golden mean is a ratio approximately equal to
1.618 that is found in nature, art, and architecture, associated
with aesthetically pleasing proportions. In web and graphic
design, using the golden mean can enhance visual appeal and
potentially lead to better user engagement as it aligns with
innate preferences for beauty in our brains.
5.Question
How can incorporating rich media enhance a website's
effectiveness?
Answer:Adding rich media such as images and videos can
significantly boost user engagement. Research indicates that
universal search results that include these media are more
engaging and draw more attention than text-only results.
Therefore, optimizing a site for rich media not only helps
engage visitors but may also provide SEO benefits by
improving search ranking.
Chapter 20 | 20| Q&A
1.Question
Why is the reciprocity strategy more effective than a
reward strategy for gathering user information?

Answer:The reciprocity strategy is more effective
because it allows users to access valuable content
first, which builds goodwill and trust. Research
indicates that users are twice as likely to provide
their contact data after experiencing the content, as
compared to when they're confronted with a
mandatory form at the start. This approach involves
giving first, which stimulates a sense of obligation in
the user to reciprocate by sharing their information.
2.Question
How can scarcity be effectively communicated in
e-commerce?
Answer:Scarcity can be effectively communicated by
providing specific information about product availability,
such as stating 'Only 2 left at this price.' Websites can
enhance urgency by showing real-time stock levels and
alerts, such as 'Only 4 left in stock—order soon!' This
specificity helps create a credible sense of urgency,
prompting quicker customer decision-making.

3.Question
What advice is given for marketing to baby boomers and
seniors?
Answer:When marketing to baby boomers and seniors, it's
crucial to keep the messaging simple and clear. This includes
using an uncluttered layout, avoiding distractions, and
providing enough white space around the message. Research
indicates that older adults struggle more with processing
distractions, so a streamlined approach enhances their
engagement and comprehension.
4.Question
What is ownership imagery, and how does it impact
online sales?
Answer:Ownership imagery is a technique that enhances
customers' perceived ownership of a product, even without
physical touch. Studies show that prompting customers to
imagine how they would use or display a product leads to
increased feelings of ownership and a higher willingness to
pay. This can be achieved through targeted questions in

product descriptions or interactive visualizations that engage
potential buyers.
5.Question
Where should a brand logo be positioned for maximum
visibility and impact in advertising?
Answer:The best placement for a brand logo is in the lower
middle part of an advertisement or webpage. This positioning
allows the viewer to have engaged emotionally with the
content before seeing the brand, facilitating a connection
between the brand and the solution to their needs.
6.Question
How can businesses create a more human-like interaction
in automated systems?
Answer:Businesses can improve automated interactions by
adopting strategies that make the computer feel more
personable. This includes forming a 'team' with users by
personalizing the interface, positioning the automation as
supportive rather than demanding, and giving the computer a
'specialist' function, which increases its credibility and the

user's trust in it.
